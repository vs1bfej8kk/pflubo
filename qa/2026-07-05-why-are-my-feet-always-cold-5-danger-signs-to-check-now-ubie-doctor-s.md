Why are my feet always cold?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Cold feet often signal reduced blood flow to the extremities. Common causes include poor circulation, Raynaud's phenomenon, acrocyanosis, peripheral artery disease, anemia, hypothyroidism, diabetes, and neurological conditions like Parkinson's disease. Persistently cold feet—especially with numbness, color changes, or pain—may indicate an underlying condition that warrants attention.

Explanation

[Danger Signs](https://dermaloom.snaketongueofficial.com/blog/9668789041.html)

Cold feet can be a common issue for many people and may be due to several reasons. One reason could be poor blood flow to the feet, which makes them colder than the rest of the body. This can happen even in healthy people, as the body tries to keep vital organs warm by reducing blood flow to the extremities, such as the feet.

In some cases, cold feet might be linked to medical conditions. For example, people with Parkinson's disease can experience very cold lower limbs. This happens because Parkinson's can affect the nervous system, which in turn affects how blood circulates in the body.

Another condition that might cause cold feet is acrocyanosis. This is where the skin, especially on the hands and feet, turns blue and feels cold. Acrocyanosis happens because of small blood vessels in the skin narrowing, which limits blood flow to these areas.

If your feet are always cold and it's uncomfortable or concerning, you can use a free symptom checker to help identify potential causes and determine whether you should talk to a healthcare provider.
