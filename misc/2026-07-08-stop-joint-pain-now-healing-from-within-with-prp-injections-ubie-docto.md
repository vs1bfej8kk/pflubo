Explanation

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy has gained traction as a natural, regenerative approach to joint pain. Often dubbed "PRP for joints," this treatment uses components from your own blood to stimulate healing. Below, we explore what PRP is, how it works, who's a good candidate, potential benefits and risks, and what to expect before, during, and after the procedure.

What Is PRP?

PRP stands for platelet-rich plasma. Platelets are blood cells best known for their role in clotting, but they also contain growth factors and proteins that aid tissue repair. The PRP process involves:

Drawing a small amount of your blood.

Spinning it in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets.

Extracting the platelet-rich layer for injection into the injured joint.

Because PRP uses your own blood, the risk of allergic reaction or disease transmission is extremely low.

How PRP for Joints Works

When injected into a joint—such as the knee, shoulder, hip, or ankle—PRP may:

Deliver growth factors that reduce inflammation and encourage tissue regeneration.

Attract stem cells and healing proteins to the site of injury.

Promote the repair of cartilage, tendons, and ligaments.

This process aims to address the root cause of joint pain rather than simply masking symptoms.

Osteoarthritis (particularly in the knee)

Many patients turn to PRP when conservative measures—like physical therapy or anti-inflammatory medications—provide only limited relief.

The PRP Injection Procedure

Understanding the steps can ease any anxiety about the process:

Consultation and Assessment

Your doctor reviews your medical history, imaging results (X-rays or MRI), and current symptoms.

Blood tests ensure you have a healthy platelet count and rule out infection or clotting disorders.

Blood Draw and Preparation

A small vial of blood (around 30–60 mL) is drawn from your arm.

The sample is placed in a centrifuge for 10–15 minutes to separate platelets from red blood cells.

Injection

The treatment area is cleaned and numbed with a local anesthetic.

Using imaging guidance (ultrasound or X-ray), your doctor injects the PRP directly into the joint or injured tissue.

Post-Injection Care

You may rest the joint for 24–48 hours.

Some mild soreness or swelling is normal and usually resolves within a few days.

Potential Benefits

Clinical studies and patient-reported outcomes suggest several advantages of PRP for joints:

Reduced pain and improved joint function

Slower progression of cartilage degeneration in osteoarthritis

Enhanced healing of tendons and ligaments

Minimal risk of allergic reaction, since PRP is autologous (from your own body)

Possibly fewer side effects compared to long-term NSAID or steroid use

Key Benefits at a Glance

Natural, non-pharmacologic approach

Stimulates your body's own healing mechanisms

Can be repeated every 6–12 months if needed

Short outpatient procedure (30–60 minutes total)

Risks and Considerations

No treatment is without potential drawbacks. Before opting for PRP for joints, consider:

Variable Results: Not everyone experiences significant relief. Success may depend on age, activity level, severity of joint damage, and overall health.

Multiple Sessions: Some protocols recommend 2–3 injections spaced weeks apart.

Cost: Many insurance plans consider PRP experimental and may not cover it. Out-of-pocket expenses can range from several hundred to over a thousand dollars per injection.

Short-Term Pain: Mild pain, swelling, or bruising at the injection site is common but usually temporary.

Lack of Standardization: Techniques, platelet concentrations, and injection protocols vary between clinics.

Who Is a Good Candidate?

PRP for joints may be most effective for:

Adults with mild to moderate osteoarthritis (grades I–II)

Patients under 65 with early cartilage changes

Individuals with chronic tendon injuries unresponsive to rest and physical therapy

Healthy non-smokers (smoking can impair healing)

Those seeking to reduce reliance on pain medications

Patients with advanced joint degeneration (bone-on-bone arthritis), active infections, or bleeding disorders may not be ideal candidates.

What to Expect After PRP Injections

Recovery and results can vary, but most patients follow a similar pattern:

First 48–72 Hours

Rest the treated joint.

Use ice packs and over-the-counter acetaminophen (avoid NSAIDs like ibuprofen, which can blunt the PRP effect).

Weeks 1–4

Gradually resume low-impact activities (walking, swimming).

Begin or continue physical therapy to strengthen surrounding muscles.
