Spoiler alert: It’s probably not. Autism spectrum disorder manifests itself in early childhood—usually around age two—and you can’t suddenly develop it when you’re an adult. Children with autism have challenges with language development. They may not respond to their name or may stop talking altogether for a period of time. Early signs also include unusual play patterns like carrying around toys and never playing with them or being uninterested in making friends with other kids. Some people, however, manage to get by and aren’t diagnosed until they’re older. They may seek help when their lives become more complex and they begin having problems at home or work. Hearing you have autism spectrum disorder can be tough, though it’s not unusual for adults who are diagnosed with it to also feel a sense of relief at finally having an explanation for certain feelings and experiences. Yet the chance that you would be diagnosed is pretty small; only about 1 percent of the worldwide population meets the clinical criteria for being anywhere on the spectrum.

While Bryce felt like he didn’t always fit in, he was happily married, he had a small group of friends, and he never really had trouble getting by at work. Like most of my patients who think they have autism, Bryce didn’t fit the criteria. Yes, being a little awkward sometimes could mean you’re on the spectrum, but most likely it just means you’re human.

People like Bryce sometimes think they could also have social anxiety disorder, and that’s a reasonable leap. It can share some characteristics with autism, like avoiding people’s eyes and socially withdrawing. But social anxiety disorder has tells, too, including an overwhelming fear of being humiliated. While you want to be around people, your anxiety is keeping you from it. That’s different from autism, where you’re less likely to be aware of social cues. Bryce didn’t meet the criteria for social anxiety disorder, either. Yet he still found ways to manage eventually.

Unpacking What “Awkward” Really Means

Telling my patients that they’re not on the spectrum and that they don’t have social anxiety disorder isn’t always the consolation you think it might be. That’s because the next thought goes something like, If I don’t have a diagnosable issue, then…I’m just awkward?

Maybe. And that’s fine. We all are awkward sometimes. Waking up tired, being in a new environment, or just having a bad day can cause us to second-guess ourselves, fumble our words, or gaze off into space. Occasional anxiety can also make you feel nervous and not as socially savvy as you’d like. Resist pathologizing—turning what you’re experiencing into something that has a diagnosis and needs treatment—simply because you’re different. You can be perfectly mentally healthy and be awkward at times.

Also know that just because you think you’re awkward doesn’t mean the people around you think you are. For example, some research shows that people with higher levels of anxiety tend to be more self-aware, which can cause them to feel embarrassed more easily by their social mishaps. You may assume that because you missed the punchline of a joke or sat in uncomfortable silence at a business lunch, you won’t be welcome again or that you’ll never make friends. Instead, start leaning into awkwardness rather than trying so hard to avoid it. By acknowledging the fact that we’re all different, and a little awkward at times, you can actually move beyond feeling like you have a problem. Don’t worry, this strategy isn’t as uncomfortable as it sounds.

How to Make Awkwardness Less...Awkward

First, notice when awkwardness comes up most often for you. Bryce realized that he felt least comfortable when he was under pressure to be social at the office. His coworkers were into sports and liked going to happy hour. Bryce wasn’t into those things and didn’t know what to say when the conversation turned to sports. He learned to say, “I’m just not into football,” which helped his colleagues understand why he was being quiet. They then started asking Bryce what he was into so they could know him better.

If you’re really uncomfortable at office parties (which, by the way, are often pretty awkward for everyone), find one or two people you’re at ease with at the event. Have a couple of go-to questions to get a conversation going, or poke fun at yourself if you do happen to fumble a social situation. And try listening more. Refocus the conversation on the other person with statements like “That’s interesting; tell me more” to get them talking. Don’t be afraid to ask questions like “What’s been on your mind lately?” to break the ice. Be okay with a few moments of silence in a conversation, and don’t always assume that a pause means there’s a social deficiency on your part.

If you’re thinking, Sounds good, but awkward still isn’t working for me, schedule an appointment with a mental-health professional. Maybe you have a disorder, maybe you don’t. Either way, you can learn to communicate better. Having challenging feelings doesn’t mean you have a disorder. Sometimes they can be just that—feelings—and ones that you can live with, work with, and grow from.
