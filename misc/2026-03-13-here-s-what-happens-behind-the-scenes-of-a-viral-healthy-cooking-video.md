# Here's What Happens Behind the Scenes of a Viral Healthy Cooking Video

Back in 2012, Kevin Curry launched a Tumblr called FitMenCook to build a community of healthy eaters and meal preppers. The blog also documented Curry’s own lifestyle transformation, from his “ah-ha” moment realizing he was out of shape to becoming a buff, charismatic, nutritional influencer.

Curry has invited his fans along for all of it, welcoming them into his kitchen, on grocery trips and even on globetrotting nutritional adventures. Now a full-fledged nutrition/fitness/lifestyle brand, Fit Men Cook has grown to include a website, YouTube channel, Instagram feed, smartphone app and cookbook. It has also grown beyond a one-man, one-camera operation.

Perusing recipes on Fit Men Cook, one might get the impression that beautiful, nutrient-rich dishes simply materialize in Curry’s kitchen. There seems to be no other explanation for the sheer quantity of food that emerges from one prolific home cook. But behind the scenes, Curry works tirelessly to produce food for every diet, craft targeted post-workout meals, and plot meal prep routines—delivering it all to readers in beautiful, social media-ready posts. For every “easy $5 recipe,” he (and his videographer, sound guy and a crew of other collaborators) works for hours and sometimes days, pours money and effort into every step, and carefully directs the camera away from the mess that comes with every dish.

Some vloggers and influencers plan out posts weeks in advance, but Curry prefers to post his content as he makes it, bringing the viewer into his world as he cooks through it. As one of the most open, legitimately enthusiastic figures in food blogging, Curry is the perfect person to take MensHealth.com behind the camera to see how much work goes into every single post.

If you thought being a foodie influencer was easy, just wait until you get a look at a day in Curry’s life.

Recipe videos take up to six hours to complete.

Curry typically shoots anywhere from three to eight recipes in a day, and even his short “quickie” recipes can take hours to complete, so he begins prepping long before the camera starts rolling. The evening before a shoot, he’ll roast potatoes, bake muffins and prepare anything more complicated than a 30-minute skillet fry up. The next day, he'll cut down on shoot time by starting a recipe on camera, then swapping in the finished dish instead of waiting for it to cook.

For recipes he completes in-full on camera, he prefers to pre-measure and organize his ingredients the night before. He collects ingredients for each recipe on a separate tray, allowing him to easily pull out everything for the next dish when it’s time to move on.

After an hour of shooting each recipe and 30-45 minutes of cleanup, Curry works with his editor to make sure they have all the right material at the right angles. Then he writes up a few paragraphs for the recipe on the website to teach home cooks about the dish (and please Google’s minimum word count for SEO). He coordinates with his YouTube guy to discuss thumbnails and SEO as well. “It’s a four-to-six-hour ordeal,” he says.

The influencer chef is rarely alone.

Even though Curry is the only one on camera, there are always at least three people in the room, including a videographer and a sound guy. On bigger shoots, especially branded content, he might bring in a sous chef and/or a dishwasher.

He never stops moving.

The art of multitasking allows Curry to clean his kitchen and move on to the next shoot in five minutes flat. “Right as I pull something out of the oven, even as I’m plating, the [dishes] are in the sink soaking,” he says. “As the photographer or videographer is taking shots of the food, I’m already back in the kitchen cleaning up and getting ready for the next recipe. I’ve got to wipe everything down and get ready. It’s down to a science now.”

The camera squeezes into a tiny corner for natural light.

Like a moth to the flame, Curry follows the light, which happens to be in one small corner of his space. “It’s really crammed in that corner,” he admits. “I put one of my hallway mirrors there to reflect off the light coming in to give it a balanced look. It’s right behind my treadmill where I work out, but that’s where the best lighting is. I’ve had to squeeze my entire production into that one corner.”

Tweezers make everything look better.

A 40-inch TV helps Curry closely review every image and keep an eye on himself during videos. He says, “I’m looking for a balance of color across the dish. Maybe one side of something has too much cilantro. One part you won’t see on camera is I’ve got some tweezers [to] take out little bits of cilantro. I’m also looking for grease spots.”

Getting the right feel to the images is a matter of balance, too. “I try to make it look relatable. I like a little mess in my videos because that translates well to the user, [showing them] it doesn’t have to be perfect,” Curry says. “But I don’t want too much mess where the dish looks unappetizing.”

A $5 recipe costs $30-50 to make on camera.

Even simple recipes cost more for the internet chef than for readers. Curry needs quality ingredients that will not only look good, but perform well on camera. “If I pick a natural chicken breast that doesn’t have any enhanced broth in it already versus one that’s frozen, they’re going to perform differently in the skillet,” he explains. “That speaks to the quality of the video.”

He has to buy more of each ingredient too. “I usually buy double the ingredients. I want to have some left over in case we miss a step on camera, or we need to repeat something. It’s the worst thing to be stuck in the middle of a food shoot and have to go back out to a grocery store if you need something.”

Dishes are intentionally unfinished.

There’s a secret reason professional chefs’ meals look so much better on the internet or TV: They’re slightly undermixed. “[Let’s say] you want to make a chicken salad. If you look at really good food photography, the shots of the chicken salad almost look unfinished,” Curry says. “They want to show off the ingredients of what’s in it. We don’t think about that when we’re just looking at a photo. We’re just like, ‘This looks really good.’ But really our chicken salad looks much different because it’s mixed together.” To get the same effect on video, Curry needs to prepare extra base ingredients, which he adds at the last second.

“Cheese pulls” and “syrup pours” are harder than they look.

The cheese pull, when hot cheese is stretched lavishly through the air, is perhaps the most iconic image in food porn, but Curry doesn’t just point the camera at a gooey mess to get those shots. “One of the hardest things to film is hot recipes. Anything with pulled or melting cheese,” Curry says. “It’s hard to capture but you have to get it right out of the oven to get those little bubbles in the corner or the cheese pulls. You have to work on those things.” Curry adds that it’s equally challenging to pour syrup on sweet dishes in a convincing, attractive way. “Timing is everything with those.”

He doesn’t stand on a chair to get overhead shots … anymore.

You might see a food photographer out in the wild standing on a few chairs to get the perfect overhead shot. That’s how Curry used to operate too, balancing his tallest tripod on stacks of boxes. But last year he discovered the magic of an extendable tripod, taking his game to the next level. “[It’s] like Hollywood for me,” he says.

Meal prep is the worst.

Dividing up meals into a few to-go boxes might seem like a breeze, but Curry says this simple format is actually hell on him. “My $75 meal prep videos are a nightmare. Sometimes I just don’t have it in me,” he jokes. Preparing large batches of several meals makes his kitchen an absolute mess. “Everyone loves to post their meal prep on Instagram and show all their tupperware. But I know what that really means. I know that kitchen is a complete crap show.”

Things can get messy.

Curry has had his fair share of spills. One shoot for his cookbook was especially messy. While shooting a butternut squash stir fry, Curry remembers someone had the idea to toss the stir fry high up into the air. The food went up…and inevitably came down all over the floor and Curry’s white sneakers.

Mistakes happen.

While he has learned to incorporate little missteps into recipes by adding lines in video voice-over, some reshoots are unavoidable. “One time, I didn’t notice it, but there was a fly the entire time in the video. So I look back later and I realized I had to redo the whole thing,” Curry says. “The most painful thing is if the camera cuts off and you don’t realize it, or you forget to press record and you’ve done the entire recipe. Then you have nothing. That’s happened quite a few times. It’s like someone is ripping your heart out.”

At the end of the day, all that food disappears into hungry mouths.

It pays to be Curry’s friend on shoot days. The chef often gives out finished recipes to his friends and coworkers. If no one will accept any more free food, Curry has been known to hit the streets of downtown Dallas and give it out to strangers in need.
