How long is tonsillitis contagious in adults over 65?

Viral tonsillitis in adults over 65 is typically contagious for 5–7 days after symptoms begin. Untreated strep throat, however, can remain contagious for 2–3 weeks. Once you've taken appropriate antibiotics for 24 hours and are fever-free, you're generally no longer contagious—though older adults and those who are immunosuppressed may shed the bacteria longer.

Seek prompt medical care if you experience:

High fever lasting more than 2 days

Trouble breathing or swallowing

Severe throat pain or signs of dehydration

Known strep exposure

Chronic conditions (heart or lung disease, diabetes, cirrhosis) or steroid use

Because tonsillitis in older adults can escalate quickly—and because viral and bacterial causes require very different treatments—knowing what's driving your symptoms matters. Is tonsillitis contagious in adults over 65? When it stops being contagious—and when to get checked

Tonsillitis, an inflammation of the tonsils, can be caused by viruses or bacteria (most often group A Streptococcus). In adults over 65, weakened immunity or underlying conditions may affect how long you remain contagious and when to seek medical help. Here's what you need to know in clear, common language.

1. Why older adults need special attention

Immune response slows with age.

Chronic conditions (diabetes, heart disease, liver disease, cirrhosis) can prolong infection.

Medications like steroids can suppress immunity.

Complications (abscess, blood poisoning) are more serious if diagnosis or treatment is delayed.

References:Shulman et al. (2012) provide guidelines for group A streptococcal (GAS) tonsillitis; Tsochatzis et al. (2014) discuss cirrhosis and infection risk.

4. Signs you should get checked

Older adults should see a healthcare provider if you experience:

High fever (over 101°F/38.3°C) lasting more than 2 days

Difficulty breathing or swallowing saliva

Severe throat pain unrelieved by over-the-counter painkillers

Signs of dehydration (dry mouth, little or no urination)

Stiff neck or severe headache (possible meningitis)

Swollen glands or a "hot potato" muffled voice (peritonsillar abscess)

5. Steps to reduce spreading tonsillitis

Start treatment promptly– If bacterial: finish the full antibiotic course (usually 10 days per Shulman et al. guidelines).– If viral: rest, fluids, and symptom control.

Practice good hygiene– Wash hands often with soap and water.– Use alcohol-based hand sanitizer when handwashing isn't possible.– Cover coughs and sneezes; discard tissues immediately.– Avoid sharing utensils, cups, toothbrushes.

Isolate when contagious– Stay home until fever-free for 24 hours and, if on antibiotics, until 24 hours after the first dose.– Limit close contact with family members, especially those with risk factors.

Support your immune system– Stay hydrated.– Get adequate rest.– Eat a balanced diet rich in vitamins C and D.– Discuss supplements or adjustments with your doctor.

7. When to seek immediate medical attention

Call emergency services or go to the nearest ER if you develop:

Sudden severe throat or chest pain

Difficulty breathing or swallowing liquids

High fever unresponsive to medication

Confusion, dizziness, or fainting

Signs of severe dehydration (confusion, very little urination)
