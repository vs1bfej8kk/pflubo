If you have excessive daytime sleepiness, this is what you’re missing.

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Excessive daytime sleepiness typically points to one of three root causes: poor restorative sleep, nutrient deficiencies, or an undiagnosed medical condition. The most common drivers include obstructive sleep apnea, narcolepsy, restless legs syndrome, hypothyroidism, diabetes, heart or lung disease, and liver disease with subtle brain changes.

An effective action plan includes tracking sleep patterns, improving sleep hygiene, requesting targeted lab work and a sleep study, screening for liver issues, and recognizing red-flag symptoms that require urgent care.

Explanation

If you're struggling with excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS)—that overwhelming urge to nap, trouble staying awake during routine activities, or constant mental fog—you may be missing one or more key pieces of the puzzle. While poor sleep habits often get the blame, EDS can have deeper causes: hidden sleep disorders, nutritional gaps, medical conditions (including liver issues), or even mood imbalances. Here's what to consider—and how to get to the bottom of your fatigue.

What you might be missing

Poor sleep quality or quantity

Inconsistent sleep schedule: Going to bed and waking up at different times throws off your body clock.

Fragmented sleep: Frequent awakenings (from noise, bathroom trips or discomfort) can leave you unrefreshed.

Inadequate duration: While individual needs vary, adults generally require 7–9 hours per night.

Undiagnosed sleep disorders• Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) – Airways collapse during sleep, causing frequent snoring and brief breathing pauses. – Daytime tiredness, morning headaches or dry mouth are red flags.• Narcolepsy – Sudden sleep attacks, cataplexy (muscle weakness), or vivid hallucinations at sleep onset.• Restless legs syndrome (RLS) – Uncomfortable leg sensations that worsen at rest, leading to toss-and-turn nights.

Nutritional deficiencies• Iron – Iron-deficiency anemia reduces oxygen transport, causing fatigue and brain fog. – A simple blood test can check ferritin and hemoglobin levels.• Vitamin D – Low vitamin D links to poor sleep quality and daytime tiredness. – Especially common in people with limited sun exposure.• B vitamins (B12, B6) – Vital for nerve function and energy production. Deficiencies can manifest as lethargy.

Mood and mind• Depression or anxiety – Both conditions can disrupt sleep architecture and leave you drained. – Persistent feelings of sadness or worry warrant professional support.• Stress and over-stimulation – Excess screen time or racing thoughts at bedtime fragment your sleep cycle.

Underlying medical conditions• Hypothyroidism – An underactive thyroid slows metabolism, making you feel sluggish. – A blood test (TSH, free T4) can provide clarity.• Diabetes – Blood-sugar swings, nocturia (nighttime urination) and nerve pain can disturb sleep.• Heart or lung disease – Chronic conditions that impair breathing or circulation often lead to unrefreshed sleep.• Liver disease and minimal hepatic encephalopathy – People with cirrhosis can develop subtle changes in brain function, leading to EDS. – D'Amico et al. highlight how fatigue and sleepiness impact prognosis in cirrhosis. – Castera et al. recommend non-invasive tools (transient elastography) to check for liver fibrosis before symptoms worsen. – According to EASL guidelines (Angeli et al.), early detection of decompensated liver disease—including sleep disturbances—can improve outcomes.

When to seek professional help

• You still feel overwhelmingly sleepy during routine activities—even after improving sleep hygiene.• You experience alarming symptoms: chest pain, shortness of breath, severe daytime confusion or falling asleep in dangerous situations (while driving).• You have signs of advanced liver disease (jaundice, abdominal swelling, easy bruising) or another serious illness.
