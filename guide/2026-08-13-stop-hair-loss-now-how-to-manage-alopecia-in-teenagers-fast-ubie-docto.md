Internal Medicine

Patchy hair loss in teens is often caused by alopecia areata, but other conditions—tinea capitis, telogen effluvium, traction alopecia, trichotillomania, or nutritional deficiencies—must be ruled out by a clinician. Treatment is typically dermatologist-directed, starting with topical or intralesional steroids, and may progress to topical immunotherapy, short-course oral steroids, or newer JAK inhibitors for extensive cases. Supportive care includes gentle hair handling, balanced nutrition, stress management, and cosmetic aids. Seek prompt medical attention for rapid spreading, signs of infection, nail or eyelash involvement, or emotional distress.

Hair loss can be alarming at any age, but when it happens in teens—often at a time of heightened self-awareness—it can feel especially stressful. Alopecia areata is one of the most common causes of patchy hair loss in teenagers. Here's a practical guide to understanding, treating, and coping with teen hair loss, based on current clinical insights and everyday strategies.

What Is Alopecia Areata?

Autoimmune condition: The immune system mistakenly attacks hair follicles, leading to smooth, round patches of hair loss.

Onset in youth: Roughly 25% of alopecia areata cases begin before age 20.[Kim & Lee, 2016]

Variable course: Some teens experience spontaneous regrowth; others have recurring or extensive hair loss.

Recognizing the Signs

Sudden appearance of one or more round, smooth patches of hair loss

"Exclamation point" hairs: short broken hairs that taper at the base

Nail changes (pitting, ridging) in some cases

Other Causes of Teen Hair Loss

Before concluding alopecia areata, it's important to consider and rule out other common causes:

Telogen effluvium: Diffuse shedding after stress, illness, or rapid weight change

Tinea capitis: Fungal scalp infection—may show redness, scaling, or black dots

Traction alopecia: Hair breakage from tight hairstyles

Trichotillomania: Hair pulling linked to stress or behavioral issues

Nutritional deficiencies: Iron, vitamin D, or protein deficits (though true deficiency is less common)

Each treatment carries potential benefits and risks. A dermatologist can tailor a plan based on the extent of hair loss, teen's age, and overall health.

Lifestyle and Supportive Strategies

Hair and Scalp Care

Use gentle, sulfate-free shampoos and conditioners

Avoid tight hairstyles (braids, buns) that pull on hair follicles

Limit heat styling, bleaching, or harsh chemical treatments

Nutrition

Lean proteins (eggs, fish, legumes)

Iron sources (spinach, lean red meat)

Vitamins D and B complex from whole foods

Supplement only if a blood test confirms deficiency

Stress Management

Stress can exacerbate autoimmune flares

Mindfulness or relaxation exercises

Regular physical activity

Adequate sleep (8–10 hours for teens)

Coping and Emotional Well-Being

Teen hair loss can affect self-esteem and social life. Addressing the emotional side is as crucial as medical care.

Consider counseling or therapy for body-image concerns

Join support groups (in person or online) to connect with peers facing similar challenges

Wigs, scarves, or hats for special events

Camouflage powders or fibers to blend bald patches
