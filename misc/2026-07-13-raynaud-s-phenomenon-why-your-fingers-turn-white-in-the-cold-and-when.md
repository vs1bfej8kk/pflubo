# Raynaud's Phenomenon: Why Your Fingers Turn White in the Cold and When Doctors Investigate Further | Ubie Doctor's Note

Raynaud's Phenomenon: Why Your Fingers Turn White in the Cold and When Doctors Investigate Further

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Raynaud's phenomenon is a circulation disorder in which small blood vessels in the fingers and toes constrict excessively in response to cold temperatures or emotional stress. This vasospasm produces a hallmark sequence of color changes: white (pallor) as blood flow stops, blue (cyanosis) as oxygen depletes, and red (rubor) as circulation returns.

Most mild cases of Raynaud's improve with simple warming measures, stress management, and lifestyle changes such as avoiding cold exposure and quitting smoking. However, frequent attacks, severe pain, skin ulcers, or symptoms extending beyond the fingers and toes may signal secondary Raynaud's, which can be linked to autoimmune conditions like scleroderma or lupus and requires medical evaluation.

Because Raynaud's symptoms often overlap with other circulatory and autoimmune disorders, identifying which type you have is critical to choosing the right next step. What Is Raynaud's Phenomenon?

Raynaud's phenomenon refers to episodes of reduced blood flow (vasospasm) in the extremities, usually triggered by cold or emotional stress. It can affect:

Fingers and toes

Ears, nose, and lips (less common)

There are two main types:

Primary Raynaud's phenomenon– Occurs on its own without any other disease– Generally milder and more common– Often affects younger people, especially women

Secondary Raynaud's phenomenon– Occurs alongside other medical conditions (e.g., autoimmune diseases)– Can be more severe, with a higher risk of complications– May require more aggressive treatment

Why Fingers Turn White in the Cold

When you're exposed to cold air or water, your body naturally narrows blood vessels in the skin to conserve heat. In Raynaud's phenomenon, this response is exaggerated:

Cold or stress trigger– Nerve signals cause small arteries in the fingers to tighten– Blood flow decreases dramatically

Ischemic phase (white)– Skin looks pale or white due to lack of circulating blood– You may feel numbness or tingling

Deoxygenation phase (blue)– Prolonged lack of oxygen turns the skin blue or purple

Reperfusion phase (red)– When vessels reopen, blood rushes back and the skin flushes red– You might feel throbbing or burning pain

Recognizing Symptoms

People with Raynaud's phenomenon often describe episodes lasting minutes to hours. Key symptoms include:

Skin color changes: white → blue → red

Cold, numb fingers or toes during the white phase

Throbbing, stinging, or swelling when blood flow returns

In severe cases, skin sores or ulcers

When to See a Doctor

Most cases of primary Raynaud's phenomenon are harmless and managed with lifestyle changes. However, seek medical attention if you experience:

Frequent or severe attacks interfering with daily life

Signs of infection (redness, warmth, pus) or skin ulcers

Pain that doesn't improve with warming

Symptoms in areas other than fingers and toes (e.g., ears, nose)

Symptoms suggesting an underlying condition (joint pain, rash, fatigue)

Diagnosis: What Doctors Look For

To determine if you have Raynaud's phenomenon and whether it's primary or secondary, doctors typically:

Review your history and symptoms– Frequency, duration, and triggers of attacks– Family history of Raynaud's or autoimmune diseases

Examine your hands and feet– Check for color changes during or after cold exposure– Look for ulcers, thickened skin, or pitting

Perform cold challenge tests– Hands are chilled, then warmed while observing blood flow

Order blood tests– Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) to screen for autoimmune disease– Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) or C-reactive protein (CRP) for inflammation

Use nailfold capillaroscopy– A microscope examines tiny blood vessels near the fingernail to detect abnormalities

Treatment and Management

While there's no cure for Raynaud's phenomenon, many strategies can reduce attack frequency and severity.

Lifestyle and Self-Care

Dress warmly in layers, especially gloves and warm socks

Keep your core body temperature up (hats, scarves)

Warm hands and feet gradually—avoid sudden heat

Manage stress through relaxation techniques (deep breathing, yoga)

Avoid smoking and caffeine, which constrict blood vessels

Exercise regularly to improve circulation

Medications

For more severe or frequent attacks, doctors may prescribe:

Calcium channel blockers (e.g., nifedipine) to relax blood vessels

Topical nitrates to dilate small arteries in the skin

Phosphodiesterase inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil) to improve blood flow

Vasodilators (e.g., prostaglandins) in severe secondary cases

Surgical and Interventional Options

In rare, severe cases where tissue damage occurs, options include:

Sympathectomy: cutting nerves that trigger vessel constriction

Botulinum toxin injections to reduce nerve signals

Skin grafts for nonhealing ulcers

Preventing Complications

Secondary Raynaud's has a higher risk of complications, so early recognition and treatment are key:

Monitor skin daily for ulcers or color changes

Follow up regularly with your doctor if you have an autoimmune disease

Use prescribed medications consistently

Seek immediate care for infections or nonhealing sores
