# Wellness Hackers Are Obsessing Over Blood Sugar. Should They, If They Don’t Have Diabetes?

You’ve seen the patch on pro runners’ arms and heard the hype about tiny sensors feeding steady streams of glucose data to phones: Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs) are the latest obsession for big pharma (multiple companies have products available OTC), athletes, and any guy dead set on tracking.

[They If They](https://dermaloom.snaketongueofficial.com/)

First designed for people with type 1 diabetes to replace finger sticks and help manage insulin, CGMs are now available over the counter for anyone curious about their blood sugar.

But is there any benefit to seeing how your blood sugar fluctuates throughout the day if you’re otherwise healthy? We spoke to top researchers and physicians about what the data really says and what to know if you’re trying one out.

CGM Use in Healthy Men: Where the Data Stands

[They Don Have Diabetes](https://copperglow.dns0663.com/)

First things first: There is clear, strong data that support CGM use in people with diabetes, but if you don’t have diabetes, “the data are zero,” says David Nathan, M.D., director of the Diabetes Center at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, MA. To date, no meta-analyses have looked at the impact of CGM use in healthy men.

That doesn’t mean data may not emerge someday. Christopher Kelly, M.D., a cardiologist and Men’s Health advisor, adds, “I can't say for sure that CGM use among healthy men is not helpful because there hasn't been a large negative trial either. We just don't know.”

So, there’s a lot we still need to find out. And what we do know about CGM use in healthy guys, unfortunately, isn’t all that compelling. CGMs measure fluctuations in blood sugar. Normal fluctuations in blood sugar are…normal. Spikes and dips occur because we eat episodically, and nutrient absorption is reflected in rising and falling glucose levels. For most healthy men, glucose levels stay between 70 and 140 mg/dL nearly all the time—even after dessert, says Miami-based cardiologist Adedapo Iluyomade, M.D.

Decades of research show that complications in diabetes (kidney disease, heart disease, eye disease) are tied to long-term averages in blood sugar, not just a few spikes. That’s why the gold-standard measure for blood sugar is average blood sugar (A1C), not moment-to-moment variability. In people with type 2 diabetes, CGM metrics aligned with A1C, according to a recent study from Harvard Medical School that tracked eight different CGM metrics against the gold-standard measure. But for people with prediabetes or normal blood sugar levels, various CGM metrics had almost no association with A1C levels.

Dr. Nathan adds that he has yet to see any data suggesting that real-time fluctuations in blood sugar make a difference in health for someone without diabetes, though he notes, “It's a testable hypothesis; it's just that no one's done it.”

The Case for Optimization

The argument by CGM enthusiasts is that understanding blood sugar fluctuations—even those within a healthy range—may help you better optimize your health and fitness. (If you know, for example, what foods make your blood sugar rise pretty high, maybe you'll avoid those foods and lose weight or have “better” blood sugar.)

There's an idea that beyond just keeping you healthy, they may help you function at your peak. “The data is pointing to, if we have less swing, less variability, less of that roller coaster, we feel more optimized,” says Pam Nisevich Bede, R.D., a registered dietitian with Abbott, the producer of the over-the-counter CGM Lingo. For example, some research has linked fluctuations in meal timing, sedentary time, and sleep to differences in glucose patterns—even among healthy, non-diabetic adults.

A CGM lets you see those fluctuations—how eating late, sitting more, or sleeping less changes your blood sugar the next day, and that’s one of the biggest benefits of CGM use in healthy people, she says: personalization and a look at exactly how your body is responding to life. “You start to spot trends and play detective of what's going on behind the scenes.”

It sounds promising, and data can be compelling. If you can see on your phone that a specific food sends your blood sugar sky-high, you can—and should—change your behavior, right?

Doctors’ Thoughts on CGMs

Well, it’s complicated. First, while sudden surges in blood sugar outside of your normal range are associated with weight gain and could lead to diabetes down the road, the foods that do that are usually exactly the ones you’d suspect: refined carbs, white bread, added sugar, potato chips, and the like. You probably don’t need a CGM to tell you that.

Beyond that, if you’re healthy, it might not even matter so much that you spike. “In diabetes, we know that higher post-meal spikes correlate to negative cardiovascular outcomes, but in the general population, these variations within a certain range really don't correlate with any negative outcomes,” says Dr. Iluyomade. (That still doesn’t translate to “it doesn’t matter what you eat”—a steady diet of refined carbs and added sugar isn’t great for you for other reasons, of course.)

Dr. Kelly adds that those who are likely most interested in trying out CGMs for health optimization right now (athletes, nutrition nerds) are probably also the least likely to derive significant benefits, as they’re already in tune with their bodies and generally healthy.

And even if you know how your body responds, just remember: “Just because you put data in front of people, doesn't mean they'll change what they do,” says Dr. Kelly. There was, after all, a long, hard-fought movement to make calories more visible on food packaging and menus, and there’s little evidence to suggest it has significantly changed anyone’s behaviors.

To this point, Dr. Kelly reminds that, as a physician, it's hard to give any “really direct guidance” on CGM use in healthy men simply due to the lack of clinical data. Dr. Kelly says he struggles to counsel his patients who use this technology; there are currently no guidelines on how to interpret or act on the data being collected in healthy people. And that’s a big downside.

The Problems with CGMs

A wealth of information without the tools to interpret it appropriately can be anxiety-provoking. In his practice, Dr. Iluymade has seen patients become hyper-focused on every blip and “obsessed” with tailoring their diets so that their CGM tracking is as “flat” as possible throughout the day—something that can lead to unhealthy eating practices.

Of course, there are other drawbacks to CGMs as well. For one, cost: They are about $100 for a one-month supply.

Additionally, because CGMs measure what’s called interstitial fluid between your cells, readings typically lag behind actual blood sugar by about 20 to 30 minutes. When you eat, nutrients are absorbed into your bloodstream, circulate through your body, and then reach the interstitial fluid. “People could easily start conflating or confusing the way they're feeling now with what their blood sugar is now, when in fact, that's what their blood sugar was 20 minutes ago,” says Dr. Nathan.

Metrics on OTC CGMs can be off by 10 to 20 percent, too, Dr. Nathan explains, suggesting that otherwise healthy people may see “high” or “low” readings that are actually normal and worry unnecessarily.

The Bottom Line

All in all, CGMs are a transformative, even life-saving tool for people with diabetes, particularly type 1, and if you use insulin. Otherwise, they probably won’t tell you much.

Dr. Iluyomade says that if you want to use a CGM, try it for a few weeks as a coaching tool and keep an eye on these three data points. If your glucose regulation isn't as healthy as you think it is, these might tip you off to get a blood sugar test from your doc to see if you have prediabetes or diabetes.

Mean or average glucose: if you’re healthy, it should be 98 or 99 mg/dL.

Time in range: You should spend almost all of your time between 70 and 140 mg/dL. (If you see the percentage of time in range dropping over time, you may be at risk for developing prediabetes or insulin resistance.)

Over time, you notice your blood sugar levels landing outside the 70 to 140 mg/dL range. This can be a clue to insulin resistance, the development of prediabetes, or the need for some much-needed dietary changes.

And if you’re chasing health, invest in the basics—diet, exercise, sleep, stress reduction—which are proven to improve your performance.
